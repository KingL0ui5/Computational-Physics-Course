\begin{abstract}
    This paper presents a discussion of the Variational Quantum Monte Carlo (VMC) method for generating numerical solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation for the quantum harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom, and the hydrogen molecule. A range of minimisation algorithms and Monte Carlo sampling methods are examined to find the minimum of the Hamiltonian expectation values. Approximations for the ground state energies of the hydrogen atom and molecule are computed for a range of interatomic distances. The resulting profile is fitted to a Morse potential, yielding estimates for the dissociation energy, and equilibrium bond length, $\boldsymbol{D = 0.1465 \pm 0.0040}$, and $\boldsymbol{r_0 = 1.4170 \pm 0.0175}$.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
    \IEEEPARstart{I}{\MakeLowercase{n}} many physical systems, the Schrödinger equation does not have analytical solutions, requiring the use of numerical approximation. In multi-particle systems, the dimensionality of the Hilbert space scales exponentially with the number of particles. The variational principle states that for any trial wavefunction, $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$ provides an upper bound to the true ground-state energy. Therefore, this is approximated by minimising $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$.
    
\section{Theory} \label{Theory}

    Most of the work was in evaluating the integrals in the expectation value expression. Due to their high dimensionality, standard numerical integration methods such as Newton-Cotes are insufficient, with computational expense that scales exponentially with dimension. For the case of the H$_2$ molecule, the effective dimensionality is 6. This makes stochastic sampling techniques more suited to solving this particular integral.

    To evaluate the Hamiltonian expectation value, we first consider a quantity defined as the local energy, $E_l = {\hat{H} \psi(\boldsymbol{R})}/{\psi(\boldsymbol{R})},$ where we have $\boldsymbol{R} =\{r_1, ..., r_n\}$. We can then substitute this result into the expression for $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$ given in ref~\cite{scott_computational},
    \begin{equation} \label{H_exp}
        \langle \hat{H} \rangle =
        \frac{
        \int d\boldsymbol{R}\,(|\psi(\boldsymbol{R})|^2 E_l)
        }{
        \int d\boldsymbol{R}\,(|\psi(\boldsymbol{R})|^2)
        },
    \end{equation}
    \noindent where $|\psi(\boldsymbol{R})|^2 = \psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{R})\psi(\boldsymbol{R})$. We can then define the probability distribution, $\rho(\mathbf{R}) = {|\psi(\mathbf{R})|^2}/{(\int d\mathbf{R'} \, |\psi(\mathbf{R'})|^2)}$,  such that , $\langle \hat{H} \rangle = \int d\mathbf{R} \, \rho(\mathbf{R}) E_L(\mathbf{R})$. Then we can use the Monte Carlo approximation of a probability distribution to produce,
    \begin{equation} \label{MC hamiltonian expectation}
        \langle \hat{H} \rangle \approx \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} E_l(\boldsymbol{R}^{(i)}) \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_s}}\sigma_{(E_l)},
    \end{equation}

    \noindent where $\boldsymbol{R}^{(i)}$ are random samples distributed by the PDF, $\rho$, $N_s$ is the number of samples and $\sigma_{(E_l)}$ is the standard deviation of the local energies.
    \subsection{The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator}
        The quantum harmonic oscillator has the Schrödinger equation that can be written in the dimensionless form,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{d^2\psi}{d\xi^2} + (\lambda - \xi^2)\psi = 0,
        \end{equation*}
        \noindent following the substitution $\xi = x\sqrt{(m_e \omega)/ \hbar}$, where $\lambda = {2\mathcal{E}}/{\hbar \omega}$. The Schrödinger equation has eigenstates and eigenvalues given by,
        \begin{equation} \label{QHO eigenstates}
            \psi_n(x) = H_n(x)e^{-x^{2}/2}, 
            \qquad
            E_n = n + \frac{1}{2},
        \end{equation} 
        \noindent where $H_n(x)$ are the Hermite polynomials. The local energy, $E_l$ of this system is then given by, 
        \begin{equation*}
            E_{\ell}(x) = \frac{1}{\psi(x)} \hat{H}\psi(x)
            = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\psi}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\psi(x) + \frac{1}{2}x^{2}.
        \end{equation*}

    \subsection{The Hydrogen Atom}
        In Hartree atomic units, the Hamiltonian for the Hydrogen atom under the Born-Oppenheimer approximation is,
        \begin{equation} \label{hydrogen atom hamiltonian}
            \hat{H} = -\frac{1}{2} \nabla^2 - \frac{1}{r},
        \end{equation}
        \noindent where energies are measured in Hartrees (Ha), where $E_h=m_e e^4 / [(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2 \hbar^2]$, and $r=\sqrt{x^2 +y^2 + z^2}$. We express the wavefunction parametrised in $\theta$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \psi(x,y,z; \theta) = e^{-\theta r}.
        \end{equation*}
        The value of $E_l$ at the minimum value of $\theta$ produces the ground state energy, known to be ${\langle \hat{H} \rangle_{min}} = -0.5$.

    \subsection{The Hydrogen Molecule}
        The Hamiltonian for the hydrogen molecule, in Hartree atomic units, is given by,
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{split}
            \hat{H} &= -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{2} \nabla_i^2 
                      - \sum_{i=1}^{2} \sum_{j=1}^{2} \frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{q}_j|} \\[1ex]
                    &\quad + \frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|} 
                      + \frac{1}{|\mathbf{q}_1 - \mathbf{q}_2|}
            \end{split}
            \end{equation*}

        \noindent We use an ansatz parametrised by $\theta_{1,2,3}$, derived from the Heitler–London valence-bond theory with a Jastrow factor, incorporated to satisfy the expected 'cusp' behaviour,
        \begin{equation}
            \begin{split}
                \psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \bigg[ & e^{-\theta_1 (|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{q}_1| + |\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{q}_2|)} \\
                & + e^{-\theta_1 (|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{q}_2| + |\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{q}_1|)} \bigg] e^{-\frac{\theta_2}{1+\theta_3 r_{12}}}
            \end{split}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Calculating the ground state energy is done by minimising $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$ with respect to $\theta_{1,2,3}$. Performing this minimisation over a variety of distances produces a Morse potential, 
        \begin{equation} \label{Morse Pot}
            V_{\text{Morse}}(r) = D \left( 1 - e^{-a(r-r_0)} \right)^2 - D + 2E_{\text{single}}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent where $D\approx0.17$ is the dissociation energy, $r_0\approx1.4$ is the bond length, $a$ is a fitting constant, and $E_{single}$ is the ground state energy for the hydrogen atom.

\section{Numerical Derivatives} \label{Numerical derivatives}
    The second-order finite difference methods for computing the local energy were tested first in one dimension using the simple harmonic oscillator, see fig.\ref{derivatives}. The central difference method outperformed the backward and forward difference methods, with errors approximately 3x smaller, with the same optimal stepsize. Thus, the central difference method was used.

    Tests for first derivatives for the H$_2$ molecule, and second derivatives in 3 dimensions for the hydrogen atom, produced similar results (fig.\ref{derivatives}). In the tested cases, the optimal method was of 8th order ($O(h^8)$), with little runtime difference.  This justified the use of the $O(h^8)$ central difference methods everywhere.


\section{Stochastic Function Sampling} \label{sampling}
    Given their high dimensionality and nonlinearity, the wavefunctions we consider cannot be directly sampled; thus, they are well-suited to rejection-based Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) type sampling methods.
    
    \begin{figure}[t] 
        \raggedright
        \hspace*{-25pt}
        \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth, keepaspectratio]{Report/Figures/QHO_sampler_distributions.png} 
        \caption{QHO ($n=3$) samples (2.1) MH, (2.2) MALA vs sMALA. Note how sMALA captures the low probability regions well with a random walk $p=0.3$, all $N_s = 10^{5}$} \label{sampling QHO}
    \end{figure}
    
    \subsection{Metropolis-Hastings Sampling}
        The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) sampling algorithm was effective in the H$_2$ scenario, where the Jastrow-Slater ansatz is challenging to sample otherwise. 

        A Gaussian proposal function was used for its symmetry and ease of implementation, with a standard deviation that could be tuned to maintain the canonical acceptance rate of 0.234  ref~\cite{gelman_1997_weak}. This ensured effective sampling of the wavefunction, whilst maintaining low autocorrelation. This was also confirmed visually for all systems under consideration, see fig.\ref{sampling QHO}. 
        
        Autocorrelation quantifies the statistical independence between samples. Highly autocorrelated samples have a reduced effective sample size, $N_{eff} = N_s / 2\tau$ where $\tau$ is the autocorrelation time. Standard error scales as  $\sigma_{(E_l)} \propto 1/\sqrt{N_{eff}}$, so it is important to keep this low. To reduce this, thinning was used - though at the expense of fewer samples for a given runtime. 

        The number of samples, $N_s$, affects both the numerical error and runtime of the simulation. Visually, sample sizes below $10^4$ produced distributions with slight deviation from the true function, whilst sample sizes in excess of $10^5$ represented it well, see fig.\ref{sampling QHO}. Therefore, $N_s = 10^5$ was used in the majority of cases considered here. 

        A burn-in period was also removed from the start of the sampled points, preventing bias from the starting configuration in the final sampled distribution. This burn-in period would always exceed the correlation time, $\tau$, as to lose correlation with the starting configuration.

    \subsection{Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm}
        Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) was also implemented. Standard Metropolis sampling uses a "random walk", whilst MALA uses the gradient of the wavefunction to propose moves towards regions of higher probability. This gives MALA desirable mixing properties, though it is more computationally expensive.

        Proposals are generated using a Langevin equation, which introduces a drift force based on the wavefunction gradient to guide the random walk,
        \begin{equation}
            \mathbf{R}_{n+1} = \mathbf{R}_n + \Delta \mathbf{f}_n + \sqrt{2T\Delta}\,\mathbf{z}_n
        \end{equation} 
        \noindent where $\mathbf{R}_n$ is the electron configuration at step $n$, $\Delta$ is the time-step, and $\mathbf{z}_n$ is a random vector drawn from a standard normal distribution, ref.~\cite{becca_2017_quantum}. In this context, $T$ represents the diffusion coefficient ($=1/2$ in a.u.), and $\mathbf{f}_n$ is the drift force, $\mathbf{f}_n \coloneq {\nabla \psi(\mathbf{R}_n)}/{\psi(\mathbf{R}_n)}$.
        
        The functions considered were best resolved with $N_s = 10^5$ but to achieve the optimal acceptance of $\approx 0.54$ given by ref.~\cite{roberts_1998_optimal}, the timestep, $\Delta$ requires some tuning.

        Though this method undersamples low-probability regions (as in fig.\ref{sampling QHO}), its efficacy is maintained for calculating the local energy by preferentially sampling regions that contribute more significantly. MALA mostly outperforms MH by acceptance rate and autocorrelation unless MH is thinned.

        Mode locking occurs when this method is used, where the sampler becomes trapped in a single peak, which is problematic in the H$_2$ case. The undersampling property of low probability regions often produces low estimates of $E_l$ so an adaptation of this method was implemented, called the Stochastic Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (sMALA), where a proportion of proposals are generated via a MH random walk. The proportion is limited to 0.3 to mitigate mode-locking and encourage the sampling of low probability regions.
        
    \subsection{Other Methods}   
        Another example of a conceptually viable algorithm choice would be rejection sampling and its associated variants. For a 6-dimensional, two-electron system, finding an efficient bounding function that hugs the electron density tightly is difficult and not worth the time.
        
\section{Minimiser Functions} \label{minimisers}
    For a well-behaved parameter space like that in the hydrogen atom, convergence could be achieved using basic methods. For the H$_2$ model, achieving convergence was challenging due to a higher-dimensional parameter space. Simulated annealing (SA) was also used as a minimiser however, the selection of the annealing schedule and parameters was mostly heuristic, making it difficult to generalise. The stopping condition was based on the standard deviation of the last ten energies due to noisy convergence in every minimiser. 

    \subsection{Gradient Descent} \label{gradient descent}
        Basic gradient descent (GR) moves in the direction of steepest descent, found using the negative gradient of the objective function. In this algorithm, the 'learning rate', $\alpha$, must be tuned to determine the magnitude of each step. This is true for all variants of GD, and we adjust according to the magnitude of the gradient,
        \begin{equation} \label{learning_rate}
            \alpha_i = \alpha \, \min\!\left( 1,\; \max\!\left( 0.1,\; \lVert \nabla f \rVert \right) \right),
        \end{equation}
        which allows the method to converge more closely on the minimum, whilst preventing it from diverging with extreme gradients.

        Another variant of GD is the RMSProp algorithm, in which the learning rate is divided by a running average of recent gradients. The running average is calculated,
        \begin{equation*}
            v(w, t) := \beta v(w, t-1) + (1-\beta) (\nabla Q_i(w))^2,
        \end{equation*}
        where $\beta$ is the forgetting factor. The learning rate, $\alpha$ is then adjusted by $\alpha_i = \alpha_{i-1}/\sqrt{v(w,t)}$. This method stabilises the gradient updates despite high noise in the calculation, making it useful for our models. 

    \subsection{Quasi-Newton}
        As Quasi-Newton (QN) is still a gradient-based method, the learning rate is tuned in the same manner as in GD. The two best Hessian approximations for this implementation are the Davidon–Fletcher–Powell symmetric update (DFP) and the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno method (BFGS), 
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{cases} 
                H_{k+1} = H_k - \frac{H_k y_k y_k^\top H_k}{y_k^\top H_k y_k} + \frac{s_k s_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}, \text{(DFP)} \\[10pt]
                H_{k+1} = \left(I - \frac{s_k y_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}\right) H_k \left(I - \frac{y_k s_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}\right) + \frac{s_k s_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k},  \text{(BFGS)}
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}

        \noindent where $y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$, is the difference in gradients and $s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k$, is the difference in parameters. The parameters are updated using the Hessian applied to the current gradient. BFGS is known to outperform DFP in stochastic contexts, as DFP is sensitive to inexact line searches for gradient updates, while BFGS has much better self-correcting properties. 
        
    \subsection{Stochastic Reconfiguration}
        Due to the Jastrow factor in the wavefunction ansatz, the parameters $\theta_{2,3}$ have a more complex relationship than standard GD variants can account for, necessitating the use of the Stochastic Reconfiguration (SR) metric. 
        The gradient update used is given by ref.~\cite{becca_2017_quantum}, 
        \[
        \delta\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \Delta\, S^{-1}\mathbf{f},
        \]
        where $f$ is the negative gradient of the energy with respect to the parameters, $\Delta (\coloneq 1/2\mu)$ is the learning rate, and $S_{kk'} = \langle \mathcal{O}_k \mathcal{O}_{k'} \rangle - \langle \mathcal{O}_k \rangle \langle \mathcal{O}_{k'} \rangle$ is the matrix of logarithmic derivatives, $\mathcal{O}_k(x) = \partial_{\alpha_k} \ln \Psi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(x)$. The matrix S is inverted using LU decomposition packaged in numpy.linalg for ease.

        The parameter space metric effectively decouples the parameters in the Jastrow factor, resulting in better convergence properties. 
        

\section{Computational Process}    
    \subsection{The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator}      
        To determine the local energies of $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$, MALA sampling was used. Though low-probability regions were undersampled compared to standard MH, MALA sampled the areas contributing most to the energy evaluation well. Fig \ref{sampling QHO} shows examples of such sampling for eigenstate $n=3$. 
        
        If the initial guess was at a node of the function (where $\psi(x) \approx 0$), MALA struggled as the drift force, $F(x) \rightarrow \infty$. This pushed the proposal outside the domain of the function, resulting in all subsequent proposals being rejected immediately, producing an acceptance of $0.0$. This was resolved for the cases considered here by guessing intelligently away from nodes of the functions, $x_0=1.5$. The correlation time was calculated to be $\tau \approx 11$ steps, motivating the choice of a burn-in period of, $20$ steps.
        
        Furthermore, the timestep was set to optimise the acceptance rate as discussed in section.\ref{sampling}, producing $\Delta=0.15$.
        
    \subsection{The Hydrogen Atom}        
        For this investigation, the sampling and differentiation algorithms were extended to three dimensions (see fig.\ref{H_samples} and fig.\ref{derivatives}). By differentiating eq.\ref{H_exp} w.r.t $\theta$ it is possible to obtain the gradient of $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$,
        \begin{equation} \label{H_log_gradient}
            \frac{\partial \langle \hat{H} \rangle}{\partial \theta_k} \approx \frac{2}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \left[ \left( E_L(\mathbf{R}^{(i)}) - \langle \hat{H} \rangle \right) \frac{\partial \ln \Psi(\mathbf{R}^{(i)})}{\partial \theta_k} \right].
        \end{equation}
        This form of $\partial_\theta \langle \hat{H} \rangle$ produced much better convergence than the definition given by ref.~\cite{scott_computational}, as only one evaluation of $E_l$ is required. The derivatives here are computed using the methods discussed in section.~\ref{Numerical derivatives}.
        
        The minimiser here was stopped after a set number of iterations, rather than on the stability of the last few energy values, allowing for the most precision from the oscillation error to be gained. The error was calculated using the standard deviation of the last 10 points, summed with the standard error of the MCMC samples on the local energy. To account for autocorrelation of the samplers, $N_s$ in \ref{MC hamiltonian expectation} is replaced with the effective sample size, $N_{eff}$.

        For the random samplers, MH was suitable. sMALA did not provide any improved accuracy on the $E_l$ calculation despite its longer runtime. The starting point was initialised to $x_0=1$ for each coordinate, with a burn-in time $50$, resulting from the correlation time, $\tau=32$.

\section{Conclusion}
    Using the quantum harmonic oscillator as a benchmark, this paper has presented an analysis of methods used to sample from and minimise the parametrised wavefunction of the hydrogen atom to determine its ground state energy. This was extended to the hydrogen molecule, where the determination of ground-state energies using the stochastic recombination minimiser for different interatomic distances produced a Morse potential. For future scope, some element of dynamic minimiser parameter tuning should be included to help avoid convergence on local minima.
