\begin{abstract}
    This paper presents a discussion of the Variational Quantum Monte Carlo (VMC) method for generating numerical solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation for the quantum harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom, and the hydrogen molecule. A range of minimisation algorithms and Monte Carlo sampling methods are examined to find the variational minimum of the Hamiltonian expectation values. Using these methods, numerical approximations for the ground state energies of the hydrogen atom and the hydrogen molecule are computed for a range of interatomic distances. The resulting energy profile for $H_2$ at different distances $r_0$ is fitted to a Morse potential, yielding estimates for the equilibrium bond length and dissociation energy. 
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
    \IEEEPARstart{I}{\MakeLowercase{n}} many physical systems, the Schrödinger equation does not have analytical solutions, requiring the use of numerical approximation. Furthermore, in many multi-particle systems, the dimensionality of the Hilbert space scales exponentially with the number of particles. Variational Quantum Monte Carlo (VMC) is a useful technique for extracting energy predictions from the Schrödinger Equation in these cases by stochastic means. In many cases, it is most useful to determine the ground state and its associated energy. The variational principle [REF variational principle] states that for any trial wavefunction, the expectation value of the Hamiltonian provides an upper bound to the true ground-state energy. This is therefore approximated by minimising the expectation value of the Hamiltonian operator, 
    \begin{equation} \label{hamiltonian expectation value}
        \langle \hat{H} \rangle =
        \frac{
        \int d^{3}r_{1} \ldots d^{3}r_{N}\,\psi^{*}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})\,H\psi(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})
        }{
        \int d^{3}r_{1} \ldots d^{3}r_{N}\,\psi^{*}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})\,\psi(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})
        }.
    \end{equation}  

    This paper will first consider the cases of the quantum harmonic oscillator, followed by the hydrogen atom, both of which have analytic solutions suitable for validation of the method. Finally, the case of the Hydrogen Molecule is considered, whose treatment demands further computational analysis.

\section{Theory} \label{Theory}
    The ground state can be approximated by proposing a general, parametrised ansatz for the wavefunction and minimising its associated energy with respect to the parameters. The wavefunctions associated energy at its minimum will be the ground state energy.

    Most of the work involved lies in evaluating the integrals in Eq.~\ref{hamiltonian expectation value}. Due to their high dimensionality, standard numerical integration methods such as Newton-Cotes are insufficient, having computational expense that scales exponentially with dimension. For the case of the H$_2$ molecule, the effective dimensionality is 6. This makes stochastic sampling techniques far more suited to solving this particular integral.

    To derive the method used to evaluate the Hamiltonian expectation value, we first consider a quantity defined as the local energy,
    \begin{equation} \label{local energy}
        E_l = \frac{\hat{H} \psi(\boldsymbol{R})}{\psi(\boldsymbol{R})},
    \end{equation} 
    \noindent where we have abbreviated $\{r_1, ..., r_n\}$ as $\boldsymbol{R}$. We can then substitute this result back into Eq.~\ref{hamiltonian expectation value}, resulting in, 
    \begin{equation} \label{H_exp}
        \langle \hat{H} \rangle =
        \frac{
        \int d\boldsymbol{R}\,(|\psi(\boldsymbol{R})|^2 E_l)
        }{
        \int d\boldsymbol{R}\,(|\psi(\boldsymbol{R})|^2)
        },
    \end{equation}
    \noindent where $|\psi(\boldsymbol{R})|^2 = \psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{R})\psi(\boldsymbol{R})$. We can then define a probability distribution, 
    \begin{equation*}
        \rho(\mathbf{R}) = \frac{|\psi(\mathbf{R})|^2}{\int d\mathbf{R'} \, |\psi(\mathbf{R'})|^2},
    \end{equation*}
    \noindent such that the relation is satisfied,
    \begin{equation*}
        \langle \hat{H} \rangle = \int d\mathbf{R} \, \rho(\mathbf{R}) E_L(\mathbf{R}).
    \end{equation*}
    \noindent Then, following the treatment of [REF], we can use the Monte Carlo approximation of a probability distribution to produce,
    \begin{equation} \label{MC hamiltonian expectation}
        \langle \hat{H} \rangle \approx \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} E_l(\boldsymbol{R}^{(i)}) \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_s}}\sigma_{(E_l)},
    \end{equation}

    \noindent where $\boldsymbol{R}^{(i)}$ are random samples distributed by the PDF, $\rho$, $N_s$ is the number of samples and $\sigma_{(E_l)}$ is the standard deviation of the local energies.
    \subsection{The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator}
    
        \noindent In these works, the quantum harmonic oscillator is used as a benchmark system to validate sampling procedures and local energy approximations. It has the Schrödinger equation,
        \begin{equation} \label{QHO Schrodinger}
            -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2 m_{e}} \frac{d^{2}}{d x^{2}} \psi(x)
            + \frac{1}{2} m_{e} \omega^{2} x^{2} \psi(x)
            = \mathcal{E}\,\psi(x),
        \end{equation}
        \noindent with a Hamiltonian that can be written in the dimensionless form,
        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{H} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{d\hat{x}^{2}} + \frac{1}{2}\hat{x}^{2},
        \end{equation*}
        \noindent following the substitution $\hat{x} = \sqrt{({\hbar}/{m_e \omega})} \,x$, with the natural length of the system, $x_0 = \sqrt{(\hbar / m_e \omega})$.

        This Hamiltonian has eigenstates and eigenvalues given by,
        \begin{equation} \label{QHO eigenstates}
            \psi_n(x) = H_n(x)e^{-x^{2}/2}, 
            \qquad
            E_n = n + \frac{1}{2},
        \end{equation} 
        \noindent where $H_n(x)$ are the Hermite polynomials. The local energy, $E_l$ of this system is then given by, 
        \begin{equation*}
            E_{\ell}(x) = \frac{1}{\psi(x)} \hat{H}\psi(x)
            = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\psi}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\psi(x) + \frac{1}{2}x^{2}.
        \end{equation*}
        \noindent The kinetic term in this expression involves a second-order derivative, which must be evaluated using finite difference techniques, the details of which will be discussed subsequently.

    \subsection{The Hydrogen Atom}
        \noindent In this section we consider the Hydrogen atom under the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. In Hartree atomic units, the Hamiltonian is,
        \begin{equation} \label{hydrogen atom hamiltonian}
            \hat{H} = -\frac{1}{2} \nabla^2 - \frac{1}{r},
        \end{equation}
        \noindent where energies are measured in Hartrees [REF hartrees], where $E_h=m_e e^4 / [(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2 \hbar^2]$, and $r=\sqrt{x^2 +y^2 + z^2}$. We proceed by expressing the parametrised wavefunction in terms of the variable $\theta$ in the ansatz,
        \begin{equation*}
            \psi(x,y,z; \theta) = e^{-\theta r},
        \end{equation*}
        \noindent matching the known form of the ground state wavefunction, $\psi_{exact} \propto e^{-Zr/a_0}$. To obtain the ground state of this function, it will need to be minimised with respect to $\theta$. The value of $E_l$ at the optimal $\theta$ should yield the ground state energy, which can be verified against the known ${\langle \hat{H} \rangle_{min}} = -0.5$.

    \subsection{The Hydrogen Molecule}
        \noindent The final component of this simulation was to calculate the ground state energy of the hydrogen molecule for various interatomic distances. The Hamiltonian, again in Hartree atomic units, is given by,
        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{H} = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{2} \nabla_i^2 - \sum_{i=1}^{2} \sum_{j=1}^{2} \frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{q}_j|} + \frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|} + \frac{1}{|\mathbf{q}_1 - \mathbf{q}_2|}
        \end{equation*}

        \noindent Here, we use a specific ansatz, parametrised by $\theta_{1,2,3}$, derived from the Heitler–London valence-bond theory to model the electronic coordinates. A Jastrow factor is incorporated to satisfy the expected 'cusp' behaviour, ensuring that the local energy remains finite at short interparticle distances: $r_{12} \rightarrow 0$, where $r_{12} = |r_1 - r_2|$.
        \begin{equation}
            \begin{split}
                \psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \bigg[ & e^{-\theta_1 (|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{q}_1| + |\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{q}_2|)} \\
                & + e^{-\theta_1 (|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{q}_2| + |\mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{q}_1|)} \bigg] e^{-\frac{\theta_2}{1+\theta_3 r_{12}}}
            \end{split}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent Similarly to before, calculating the ground state energy is a matter of minimising $\langle \hat{H} \rangle$ with respect to $\theta_{1,2,3}$. It is expected that performing this minimisation over a variety of distances should produce a Morse potential, 
        \begin{equation}
            V_{\text{Morse}}(r) = D \left( 1 - e^{-a(r-r_0)} \right)^2 - D + 2E_{\text{single}}
        \end{equation}
        \noindent where $D$ is the dissociation energy, $r_0$ is the bond length, $a$ is a fitting constant related to the width of the potential, and $E_{single}$ is the calculated ground state energy for the hydrogen atom.

\section{Numerical Derivatives} 
    Finite difference methods were tested to obtain numerical estimates of the derivatives of the functions above. The second-order methods for computing the local energy were tested first in one dimension using the simple harmonic oscillator, see fig~\ref{derivatives}. The central difference method outperformed the backward and forward difference methods, with errors approximately 3x smaller, whilst sharing the same optimal stepsize. Thus, the central difference method was adopted as the primary differentiator.

    Tests for first derivatives for the H$_2$ molecule, and second derivatives in 3 dimensions for the hydrogen atom, produced similar results~\ref{derivatives}. In the tested cases, it was clear that the optimal method was of 8th order ($O^8$), with very little runtime difference.  This justified the use of the $O^8$ central difference methods in all cases.

\section{Stochastic Function Sampling} \label{sampling}
    The methodology used to solve the systems discussed in~\ref{theory} relies on the use of function sampling to calculate the value of the wavefunction at a given coordinate in space. Given their high dimensionality and nonlinearity, the wavefunctions we consider cannot be directly sampled; thus, they are well-suited to rejection-based Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) type sampling methods.
    
    Here, we discuss the methods considered and tested for this implementation. 
    
    \subsection{Metropolis-Hastings Sampling}
        One of the principal methods used in this simulation was the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) sampling algorithm, which proved particularly effective in the H$_2$ scenario, where the Jastrow-Slater ansatz is challenging to sample by other means. Importantly, as the acceptance is determined based on a ratio, the wavefunction can be sampled without the need to calculate the non-trivial normalisation constant. % check this

        A Gaussian proposal function was used for its symmetry and ease of implementation, with a standard deviation that could be tuned to maintain the canonical acceptance rate of 0.234[REF] [ FIG]. This ensured effective sampling of the wavefunction, whilst maintaining low autocorrelation. This was also confirmed visually for all systems under consideration, see [FIG]. 
        
        For this implementation, it was crucial to reduce autocorrelation as much as possible. This quantity determines the degree of statistical independence between samples. If samples are highly autocorrelated, they have a reduced effective sample size, $N_{eff} = N_s / 2\tau$ where $\tau$ is the autocorrelation time. As the standard error scales as  $\sigma_{(E_l)} \propto 1/\sqrt{N_{eff}}$, it is important to keep this low.

        The number of steps in the sampler, $N_s$, is the main factor that directly affects both the numerical error and runtime of the simulation. Therefore, its tuning is an important consideration. From visual analysis, sample sizes below 10,000 produced distributions with slight deviation from the true function, whilst sample sizes in excess of 100,000 represented it well, see [FIG]. However, the difference in numerical error with the different sample numbers was evident. When computing the ground state energy for the Hydrogen atom, $N_s = 10,000$ produced an error of [ERROR], whilst $N_s = 100,000$ produced only [ERROR]. Therefore, $N_s = 100,000$ was used in the optimisation of the hydrogen molecule due to the necessity of accurate numerical calculations. Due to its significance, further rigorous optimisation of this parameter is warranted. 

        The use of thinning substantially reduced autocorrelation, at the expense of fewer samples for a given runtime. [FIGURE] The other benefit of rejection-based sampling in quantum systems is that there is no requirement to specify the limits on the sample coordinates, as the wavefunction PDFs are required to approach zero as $x\rightarrow\pm\infty$.

        A burn in period was also removed from the start of the sampled points, thereby preventing the starting configuration from introducing bias into the final sampled distribution. This burn in period was chosen to exceed the correlation time, $\tau$, to ensure that the memory of the initial configuration had been lost before the samples were recorded. The correlation time was determined for each system before deciding on the burn in period.

    \subsection{Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm}
        Improving upon the efficiency of the standard Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) was implemented. Standard Metropolis sampling uses a diffusive "random walk" that scales poorly with system size, whilst MALA uses the gradient of the wavefunction to propose moves towards regions of higher probability. This gives MALA better mixing properties, despite being more computationally expensive per step.

        Proposals are generated using a discretised Langevin equation, which introduces a drift force based on the wavefunction gradient to guide the random walk, 
        \begin{equation}
            \mathbf{R}_{n+1} = \mathbf{R}_n + \Delta \mathbf{f}_n + \sqrt{2T\Delta}\,\mathbf{z}_n
        \end{equation} ~\ref{MALA cambridge}
        
        \noindent where $\mathbf{R}_n$ is the electron configuration at step $n$, $\Delta$ is the time-step, and $\mathbf{z}_n$ is a random vector drawn from a standard normal distribution. In this context, $T$ represents the diffusion coefficient ($=1/2$ in a.u.), and $\mathbf{f}_n$ is the drift force, defined by the wavefunction gradient $\mathbf{f}_n \coloneq {\nabla \psi(\mathbf{R}_n)}/{\psi(\mathbf{R}_n)}$.
        
        Similarly to MH, the functions considered were best resolved with $N_s = 100,000$; however, to achieve the optimal acceptance of $\approx 0.54$ given by [MALA REF], the timestep, $\Delta$ requires some tuning.

        Though this method undersamples low-probability regions, its efficacy is maintained for calculating the local energy by preferentially sampling regions that contribute more significantly. Additionally, for particular timesteps, MALA outperforms MH by acceptance rate and autocorrelation (without thinning). [FIGURE]

        Mode locking can occur when this method is used, where the sampler becomes trapped in a single peak if the peaks are spatially separated, as is of particular concern in the H$_2$ case. Further to this, the undersampling property of low probability regions often yields spuriously low estimates of $E_l$. An adaptation of this method was therefore necessary, which we call the Stochastic Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (sMALA), in which a proportion of proposals are generated via a random walk. In this implementation, the proportion is limited to 0.4 to mitigate mode-locking, and encourage the sampling of low probability regions for all cases.
        
    \subsection{Other Methods}   
        There are also some non-trivial examples of unsuitable methods.
        
        An example of a conceptually viable algorithm choice would be rejection sampling and its associated variants. However, the need to propose an efficient overlaying proposal distribution makes it hard to implement a general algorithm whilst maintaining computational efficiency. For a 6-dimensional, two-electron system, finding a bounding function that hugs the electron density tightly is tricky. Furthermore, as the dimensionality grows, the acceptance probability falls as the volume of the proposal distribution grows exponentially; hence, the usefulness of this method is limited to low dimensions.

        Additionally, whilst the Gibbs sampling method facilitates a 100\% acceptance rate, it is not viable for this case. It requires a conditional probability distribution that is precluded by the non-linear Jastrow factor in the ansatz used for the hydrogen molecule. % potentially exclude?
        
\section{Minimiser Functions} \label{minimisers}
    The most complex part of the simulation presented here was the choice of minimiser function used to obtain the ground state energy. For a well-behaved parameter space such as that in the ansatz for the hydrogen atom situation, convergence on the minima was easily achieved using standard methods. However, for the H$_2$ system, achieving convergence proved substantially more challenging due to the increased dimensionality of the parameter space. In addition to those listed below, the method of simulated annealing was used as a minimiser, however the selection of the annealing schedule and parameters relied heavily on heuristics, making it difficult to generalise and rigorously justify.

    \subsection{Gradient Descent} \label{gradient descent}
        The simplest version of gradient descent (GR) operates by iteratively moving in the direction of steepest descent, as determined by the negative gradient of the objective function, which can be determined using the finite difference methods defined before. The main parameter to tune in this algorithm is the 'learning rate', which determines the magnitude of the motion towards the minima. This property is shared by all variants of this method, and in the present implementation, is adjusted according to the magnitude of the last calculated gradient. 
        \begin{equation} \label{learning_rate}
            \alpha_i = \alpha \, \min\!\left( 1,\; \max\!\left( 0.1,\; \lVert \nabla f \rVert \right) \right).
        \end{equation}
        This ensures that the learning rate decreases with the gradient, allowing the method to converge more closely on the minimum, whilst preventing it from diverging when the gradient is extreme.

        Another variant of gradient descent, specifically stochastic gradient descent, is the RMSProp algorithm, in which the learning rate is divided by a running average of recent gradients for that weight. [REF RMSProp] First, the running average is calculated using, 
        \begin{equation*}
            v(w, t) := \beta v(w, t-1) + (1-\beta) (\nabla Q_i(w))^2,
        \end{equation*}
        where $\beta$ is the forgetting factor. The learning rate, $\alpha$ is then adjusted by $\alpha_i = \alpha_{i-1}/\sqrt{v(w,t)}$. The running average stabilises the updates despite the high noise in the gradient calculation, making it a theoretically useful algorithm for this implementation. 

    \subsection{Quasi-Newton}
        Quasi-Newton (QN) makes iterative approximations to the Hessian matrix using a history of gradient variations to approximate the curvature of the energy surface for better gradient updates. As it is still a gradient-based method, the learning rate is tuned in the same manner as in the gradient descent method and its variants, following Eq.~\ref{learning_rate}. The two most suitable Hessian approximations for this implementation are the Davidon–Fletcher–Powell symmetric update (DFP) and the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno method (BFGS), 
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{cases} 
                H_{k+1} = H_k - \frac{H_k y_k y_k^\top H_k}{y_k^\top H_k y_k} + \frac{s_k s_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}, & \text{(DFP)} \\[10pt]
                H_{k+1} = \left(I - \frac{s_k y_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}\right) H_k \left(I - \frac{y_k s_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}\right) + \frac{s_k s_k^\top}{y_k^\top s_k}, & \text{(BFGS)}
            \end{cases}
        \end{equation*}

        \noindent where $y_k = \nabla f(x_{k+1}) - \nabla f(x_k)$, the difference in gradients and $s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k$, the difference in parameters. The parameters are then updated by the Hessian applied to the evaluated gradient. The use of Hessian approximation here produces rapid convergence of the method, while eliminating the need for noisy second derivative calculations derived from Monte Carlo samples. 
        
    \subsection{Stochastic Reconfiguration}
        The stochastic reconfiguration (SR) method can be subsumed as a form of gradient descent. Due to the inclusion of the Jastrow factor in the wavefunction ansatz, the parameters $\theta_{2,3}$ have a more complex relationship than standard Euclidean gradients can account for, necessitating the use of the Stochastic Reconfiguration (SR) metric. The derivation of this follows the treatment of [REF VMC cambridge] considers a perturbation of the wavefunction $\psi(\boldsymbol{R^{(i)};\theta}) \rightarrow \psi(\boldsymbol{R^{(i)};\theta + \delta\theta})$, and the distance introduced by the perturbation, $\delta s^2$. The perturbation is then expanded, eventually producing a quadratic form, 
        \begin{equation*}
            \delta s^2 = \sum_{k,k'} S_{k,k'} \delta\theta_k \delta\theta_{k'}
        \end{equation*}
        where,
        \begin{equation}
            S_{k,k'} \approx \text{Re} \left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(\mathcal{O}_k(x_i) - \overline{\mathcal{O}}_k\right) \left(\mathcal{O}_{k'}(x_i) - \overline{\mathcal{O}}_{k'}\right)^* \right]
        \end{equation}
        and,
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathcal{O}_k(x_i) = \frac{1}{\Psi(x_i)} \frac{\partial \Psi(x_i)}{\partial \alpha_k}
        \end{equation*}
        
        The gradient update is then given by the solution to the linear system, 
        \[
        \sum_{k'} S_{k k'}\, \delta\alpha_{k'} = \frac{f_k}{2\mu},
        \qquad
        \text{or equivalently,}
        \qquad
        \delta\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \Delta\, S^{-1}\mathbf{f},
        \]
        where $f$ is the negative gradient of the energy with respect to the parameters and $\Delta (\coloneq 1/2\mu)$ is the learning rate. In this implementation, the matrix S is inverted using... [MATRIX INVERSION METHOD]

        The parameter space metric used in this algorithm ensures that the minimiser takes physically consistent steps towards the minimum energy, in addition to effectively decoupling the parameters in the Jastrow factor, resulting in better convergence properties.

    \subsection{The Hydrogen Molecule}
        For the hydrogen molecule, the presence of two electrons increases the effective dimensionality of the sampling space to 6. Regardless, by partitioning each sampled configuration into the electron coordinates, $r_1$ and $r_2$, the remaining components of the calculation maintain their three-dimensional structure. Some examples of electron spatial probability distributions are included in [FIGURE]. It was found that sMALA sampling was far too slow for this case, producing far fewer samples for the same runtime. This was due to the significantly increased computational complexity of the wavefunction in question, resulting in much slower derivative calculations for the force term. sMALA also consistently resulted in estimates of $E_l$ that were far below what was expected for the minimum energy. This was attributed to the preferential sampling of points close to the nucleus, where the local energy diverges. This caused a skew in the expectation value $\langle E \rangle$ that made MALA and its variants unsuitable for the H$_2$ model.

        Therefore, MH sampling was used in the calculation. A thinning value of 20 was used, which would retain enough samples for an accurate approximation of $E_l$ whilst significantly reducing the autocorrelation.
        
        Once more, MH was initialised at $x=1$ for each coordinate. The samples produced a correlation time of $\tau = $ resulting in the choice of burn-in period, $burnin$.

        Furthermore, due to the expanded parameter space in $\theta_{1,2,3}$, the use of Eq.~\ref{H_partial_theta} requires 3 separate evaluations of the local energy to obtain the derivative with respect to each parameter, $\nabla_{\theta_i}$. The use of Eq.~\ref{H_partial_theta} resulted in significant noise in the derivative calculations, thereby preventing gradient-based minimisation algorithms from converging. [FIG? non convergence due to bad derivative]. Instead, a slight rearrangement of Eq.~\ref{H_partial_theta} produces, 
        \begin{equation} \label{H_log_gradient}
            \frac{\partial \langle \hat{H} \rangle}{\partial \theta_k} \approx \frac{2}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \left[ \left( E_L(\mathbf{R}^{(i)}) - \langle \hat{H} \rangle \right) \frac{\partial \ln \Psi(\mathbf{R}^{(i)}}{\partial \theta_k} \right],
        \end{equation}
        which indeed, only requires one evaluation of the local energy at the coordinates $\boldsymbol{R^{(i)}}$. This results in much less noise in the calculation of the gradient, and thus convergence of the minimisers. 

        In this system, the choice of minimiser is somewhat more complicated; [FIG] shows the convergence properties of the methods under consideration. Though simulated annealing converges slowly, its final energy is highly stable and within the expected range. However, the tuning of this method is purely heuristic, raising concerns over the validity of the parameters for other interatomic distances. The much slower convergence rate also resulted in much longer runtimes due to the requirement for one sample per iteration of the minimiser.   
        
        Otherwise, it can be seen from [FIG] that both SR and RMSProp GR have the best convergence properties, settling to a lower minimum with much less noise than other methods. SR converges remarkably rapidly, typically reaching a stable energy within less than 5 iterations, and whilst RMSProp does not converge as quickly, it settles to a reasonably stable energy.
        
        Both QN methods performed poorly. Despite converging quickly, they produced unstable $E_l$ values, with runtimes more than twice that of other methods. For the above reasons, we proceeded with the stochastic reconfiguration and simulated annealing methods to determine the morse potential.
